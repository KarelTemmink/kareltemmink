ABOUT

About me.

I didn't grow up planning to become an astrophysicist. I followed questions that interested me, and eventually found my way to the stars.

I studied physics and astronomy in Amsterdam, initially drawn to theoretical physics and the challenge of understanding complicated systems. Over time, I found that astrophysics offered an appealing combination of physical ideas, numerical work, and observations.

That combination still shapes how I work. I like building models, but I especially enjoy investigating the result that doesn't quite make sense. Sometimes it points to new physics; sometimes it reveals an assumption that needs another look.

01 / THE PATH HERE

From theory to the stars

2018–2026

PhD in astrophysics

Radboud University, Nijmegen · Onno Pols & Stephen Justham

My doctorate focused on mass transfer between binary stars: what makes it stable or unstable, how the stars and their orbits respond, and what we might observe when an interaction ends in a merger. I defended my thesis, Rites of Offering, in June 2026.

2017–2018

Data scientist

SkyGeo, Delft

Alongside my studies, I worked with large satellite-data sets using Python and Matlab. It was an opportunity to apply numerical analysis outside university research and explain technical results to people with different backgrounds.

2015–2018

Two master's degrees

University of Amsterdam · Theoretical Physics (cum laude) & Astronomy and Astrophysics

In theoretical physics, I studied tensor-network methods for open quantum systems. In astronomy, I explored white-dwarf populations and binary evolution. I received the Sander Bais Prize in 2016 for my work in theoretical physics.

2012–2015

BSc in Physics and Astronomy

University of Amsterdam

My bachelor's thesis used X-ray variability and iron-line reverberation to investigate matter close to black holes. It was my first experience of using physical models to make sense of astronomical data.

02 / HOW I WORK

Code, questions and colleagues

The work I enjoy most combines careful technical thinking with the freedom to ask difficult questions and the chance to compare ideas with other people.

01

Research and computing

I develop stellar-evolution and population models, run large sets of calculations, and build tools to analyse them. A useful model should be not just fast or detailed, but understandable and testable.

MESA · SeBa · binary_c · Python · Fortran · Linux / HPC

02

Teaching and explaining

I was a teaching assistant for courses on compact binaries and gravitational waves, and on the nuclear evolution of the Universe. I enjoy finding explanations that make difficult ideas approachable without hiding the physics.

03

Making knowledge easier to share

I've organised and chaired research-group meetings, written documentation for our computing cluster, and helped with a departmental PhD wellbeing survey. Good research depends on people being able to ask questions and share what they know.

03 / AWAY FROM THE SCREEN

Away from the desk

Outside work, I play bass guitar, experiment in the kitchen, and spend a fair amount of time in the gym. I've recently started strongman training, which has introduced me to the peculiar satisfaction of lifting and carrying very heavy, awkwardly shaped objects. I also enjoy books, music, and long walks outdoors. Learning the bass remains a useful exercise in patience and humility.

These pursuits have little to do with binary stars, which is rather the point.

04 / LOOKING OUTWARD

Beyond astrophysics

I'm interested in work that brings together modelling, data analysis and clear communication. My experience spans university research and industry, and I value teams where ideas can be challenged, technical knowledge is shared, and careful analysis leads to useful decisions.
